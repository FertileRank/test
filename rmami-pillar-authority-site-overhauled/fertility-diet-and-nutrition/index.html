Diet and Nutrition

Fertility, diet, and nutrition, supportive habits for your journey.

Nutrition is one piece of a larger fertility picture. While food alone does not determine outcomes, balanced, evidence-informed habits may support overall reproductive health. RMA of Michigan shares general guidance to discuss with your care team.

In brief

Does diet affect fertility?

Diet is one of several lifestyle factors that may influence reproductive health, alongside sleep, activity, stress, and medical factors. No single food or diet can promise pregnancy, but balanced eating patterns rich in vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats may support overall health. The most helpful nutrition plan is individualized, especially for conditions such as PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome).

Quick answer

Focus on balanced, sustainable habits rather than restrictive trends. Talk with your physician before major changes or supplements, especially if you have a condition such as PCOS or are preparing for treatment.

General guidance

Habits that may support reproductive health

These are general, educational ideas to discuss with your care team, not personalized prescriptions.

Balanced eating patterns

Vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats support overall health.

Folate and key nutrients

Prenatal vitamins with folate are commonly recommended when trying to conceive; ask your physician.

Mindful of added sugar

For some, especially with PCOS, limiting added sugar and refined carbs may help.

Hydration and moderation

Staying hydrated and moderating caffeine and alcohol are reasonable steps.

Movement and sleep

Regular activity and good sleep support overall and reproductive health.

Individualized plans

A registered dietitian can tailor guidance to your needs and goals.

Individualized care

Putting nutrition in perspective

It is easy to feel pressure to find the perfect fertility diet, but there is no magic food or plan. Nutrition is supportive, not deterministic, and balance and consistency matter more than any single ingredient.

For some conditions, such as PCOS, certain eating patterns may be more helpful, which is why individualized guidance, sometimes with a registered dietitian, can be valuable.

This page is educational and does not replace individualized medical or nutritional advice. Talk with your physician before significant changes or new supplements.

Questions to bring

  • What eating patterns might support my situation?
  • Which supplements, if any, are appropriate for me?
  • Would a referral to a dietitian help?
  • How does nutrition fit with my treatment plan?

Frequently asked

Common questions

Is there a best diet for fertility?

There is no single best diet. Balanced eating patterns rich in vegetables, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats may support overall health, but no food can promise pregnancy.

Should I take supplements?

Prenatal vitamins with folate are commonly recommended when trying to conceive, but talk with your physician before starting supplements, since needs vary.

Does nutrition matter for PCOS?

For some people with PCOS, certain eating patterns may help support more regular ovulation. Individualized guidance is most useful.

Can diet replace fertility treatment?

No. Nutrition is supportive and is one part of a larger picture. It does not replace medical evaluation or treatment.

Should I see a dietitian?

A registered dietitian can tailor guidance to your goals and any conditions. Your physician can advise whether a referral may help.

Do caffeine and alcohol affect fertility?

Moderation is generally reasonable. Your physician can give guidance based on your situation and any treatment plan.
